Homeowner guide

When to hire a licensed operator — and when exclusion is enough

Wildlife in a house is usually one of two jobs. Either animals are still using the space, or they have already left and you need to close the hole. Mixing those jobs up is how people seal young in an attic or push a raccoon into the living space.

This page is a homeowner summary, not a permit, not veterinary advice, and not a substitute for the law in your state. Ohio, New York, and Massachusetts all require a licensed commercial operator for paid wildlife work.

Hire a licensed NWCO when

  • The den is occupied. Scratching in an attic, chimney, soffit, or crawlspace means something is still coming and going. A cap or a patch installed now can trap animals inside.
  • Young may be present. Spring and early summer dens often hold nursing raccoons, squirrels, or skunks. A one-way door can separate a mother from kits. That is a licensed-operator problem, not a weekend hardware run.
  • Bats are involved. Timing, species, and exclusion method are regulated. Do not wait until dusk and swing a tennis racket. Do not seal a roost you have not had checked.
  • The species is a rabies vector. Raccoon, skunk, fox, and bat exposures are public-health issues. Do not handle the animal. If a person or pet is bitten or scratched, call your health department; the operator list is not an emergency room.
  • The animal is in a chimney, wall void, or living space and you cannot see a clear, empty exit path.

Find someone who already appears on an official list: Ohio CNWACO public listings, New York licensed NWCOs, or the Massachusetts PAC finder.

Never seal animals in

Hardware cloth and chimney caps are the right tools after the cavity is empty. Sealing first leads to animals dying in insulation, chewing a new hole into a bedroom, or coming down the damper. Confirm vacancy: no sounds for several nights, no fresh droppings, and — when a licensed operator is involved — a check for young.

State law, in brief

Rules vary. In the three states this index covers, paid wildlife control is a licensed occupation:

  • Ohio: a Commercial Nuisance Wild Animal Control Operator license (ORC 1531.40). The public PDF is opt-in; verify the current file.
  • New York: a NYSDEC Nuisance Wildlife Control Operator license. The open-data roster is the currently licensed list.
  • Massachusetts: a Problem Animal Control (PAC) license. Use MassWildlife’s finder; this site does not republish those names.

If you are doing the work on your own property, the license rules are different from hiring someone. Read the state page. Do not assume a hunting or trapping license is the same as a commercial NWCO/PAC/CNWACO credential.

DIY only after vacancy: exclusion, not capture

Once you are sure the animals are gone, close the entry with material they cannot chew. That is exclusion. It is not live-trapping, and this page does not walk through cage traps. In many states, using a live trap on wildlife — especially to relocate it — is illegal without the right license. Do not “catch and dump” raccoons down the road.
